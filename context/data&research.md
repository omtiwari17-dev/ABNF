Document purpose: A source-grounded research reference for developing AFTERLIGHT stories about human-made hardware that remains on the Moon and Mars.

Research scope in this version
- Moon: Apollo 11 and Apollo 15 hardware and experiments, based on the two NSSDCA extracts supplied for this project.
- Mars: Mars Pathfinder and Viking 1, based on the NASA mission pages supplied by the project owner.
- This is a research document, not a final story script or a complete inventory of all human-made objects on either world.
1. Research and accuracy rules
1. Distinguish between hardware deliberately left on a planetary surface, hardware that later impacted the surface, and spacecraft whose final location is unknown.
2. Do not describe an entire spacecraft as “abandoned” when only some components remained on the surface.
3. Preserve dates, measurements, mission names and scientific outcomes as stated by the cited sources.
4. Do not infer that an object is still operational simply because it remains physically present.
5. When a source does not establish the present condition or exact location of an object, say so.
6. Treat the descriptions below as factual research notes. Any future narrative, dialogue, imagined perspective or sound design must be clearly presented as creative interpretation, not historical fact.
PART A — THE MOON
2. Apollo 11 — Eagle landing site and Early Apollo Scientific Experiments Package
Mission overview
- Mission: Apollo 11
- Lunar Module: Eagle (LM-5)
- Launch date: 16 July 1969
- Lunar landing: 20 July 1969
- Landing region: Mare Tranquillitatis (Sea of Tranquility)
- Astronauts who landed: Neil A. Armstrong and Edwin E. “Buzz” Aldrin Jr.
- Command Module pilot: Michael Collins
- Source basis: User-provided NSSDCA Apollo 11 Lunar Module / EASEP research extract.
Apollo 11’s Lunar Module carried the two astronauts to the lunar surface. It also carried the Early Apollo Scientific Experiments Package (EASEP), scientific instruments and sample-collection equipment.
Hardware left on the Moon
A. Lunar Module descent stage

The Lunar Module had two stages:
- The descent stage supported the landing and carried landing equipment, propellant tanks, supplies and stowed surface equipment.
- The ascent stage housed the astronauts and lifted off from the Moon to rendezvous with the Command/Service Module.
The descent stage served as the launch platform for the ascent stage and was left on the Moon after the astronauts departed.

Important distinction: The ascent stage was not left at the landing site when the astronauts lifted off. After docking with the Command/Service Module, the Lunar Module was jettisoned into lunar orbit. The supplied source says its fate is unknown and that it is assumed to have crashed into the lunar surface sometime within the following one to four months. This is an assumption in the source, not a confirmed impact location.

B. Early Apollo Surface Experiments Package (EASEP)

EASEP was deployed by the astronauts at the Apollo 11 landing site. It was a set of scientific instruments intended to operate on the lunar surface and send data to Earth.

The supplied source describes these components and functions:
- Two solar panels supplied electrical power. EASEP could operate only during lunar daylight.
- An antenna and communications system sent data to Earth and received commands.
- A passive seismometer measured lunar seismic activity and properties of the lunar crust and interior.
- A lunar dust detector measured dust accumulation, radiation damage to solar cells, reflected infrared energy and temperatures.
- An isotope heater was part of the instrument assembly.
- A laser ranging retroreflector (LRRR) was included as part of EASEP in the source description, although it was physically separate from the main unit and required no power.
The EASEP had a total mass of 48 kg. It was deployed approximately 17 metres south of the Lunar Module. The laser ranging retroreflector was placed approximately 5 metres north-northwest of EASEP.
What happened after deployment?
- EASEP was switched on by a command from Earth at 04:40:39 UT on 21 July 1969.
- Its first operating period ended on 3 August 1969, before local lunar sunset, as power began to fall as expected.
- It was switched on again during the next lunar day.
- On 27 August 1969, it stopped accepting commands from Earth stations and the experiment was terminated.
The supplied source reports that EASEP operated normally despite temperatures exceeding the planned maximum by 30°C.
Why this hardware matters
EASEP represents an early effort to conduct scientific measurements from a fixed installation on the Moon after the astronauts had returned to Earth. Its seismometer, dust detector and laser retroreflector served different scientific purposes: studying the Moon's interior and surface environment, and enabling laser-ranging measurements.

Story angles supported by the source
- A scientific station deliberately left behind after the first crewed lunar landing.
- Instruments that continued working after the astronauts had departed.
- The contrast between the short human visit and the longer-running experiment.
- A retroreflector that did not need electrical power, unlike the main EASEP unit.
Do not claim from this source
- That every part of Eagle remains at the landing site.
- That the Apollo 11 ascent stage has a confirmed impact location.
- That EASEP is currently operating. The supplied source says the experiment was terminated in August 1969.
3. Apollo 15 — Falcon, ALSEP and lunar surface equipment
Mission overview
- Mission: Apollo 15
- Lunar Module: Falcon (LM-10)
- Launch date: 26 July 1971
- Lunar landing: 30 July 1971
- Landing region: Mare Imbrium, at the foot of the Apennine mountain range
- Astronauts who landed: David R. Scott and James B. Irwin
- Source basis: User-provided NSSDCA Apollo 15 Lunar Module / ALSEP research extract.
Apollo 15 carried a Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV), an Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Package (ALSEP), and other scientific and sample-collection equipment.

Scott and Irwin conducted three moonwalks totalling 18 hours and 35 minutes. They travelled 27.9 km and collected 77.31 kg of lunar rock and soil samples. The rover was used to explore locations within 5 km of the Lunar Module landing site.
Hardware left on the Moon
A. Lunar Module descent stage

As with other Apollo Lunar Modules, the descent stage remained on the Moon and served as the platform from which the ascent stage launched.

B. Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Package (ALSEP)

ALSEP was a collection of scientific instruments deployed at the landing site. Its central station supplied power and handled communications so experiment data could be transmitted to Earth. Instruments were connected to the central station by cables.

The Apollo 15 ALSEP instruments listed in the supplied source were:
1. Passive seismometer — measured seismic activity and properties of the lunar crust and interior.
2. Lunar surface magnetometer — measured the magnetic field at the lunar surface.
3. Solar wind spectrometer — measured the fluxes and spectra of electrons and protons from the Sun reaching the lunar surface.
4. Suprathermal ion detector — measured the flux, composition, energy and velocity of low-energy positive ions.
5. Cold cathode ion gauge — studied the lunar atmosphere and possible changes associated with time or solar activity.
6. Lunar dust detector — measured dust accumulation, radiation damage to solar cells, reflected infrared energy and temperatures.
7. Heat flow experiment — measured heat loss from the lunar interior and thermal properties of lunar material.
C. SNAP-27 Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (RTG)

The Apollo 15 ALSEP was powered by a SNAP-27 RTG. The supplied source states:
- The generator assembly had a mass of 17 kg.
- Its fuel capsule contained plutonium-238.
- Heat from radioactive decay was converted into electrical power by thermoelectric couples.
- It produced approximately 70 watts DC at 16 volts (approximately 63.5 watts after one year).
This is a description of the Apollo 15 ALSEP power system; it should not be confused with the instruments themselves.

D. Lunar Roving Vehicle and other surface items

Apollo 15 also carried and used a Lunar Roving Vehicle. The supplied extract confirms its use during the three traverses but does not provide a detailed end-of-mission description of its final condition. The source also records that the astronauts left a plaque and a small memorial figure on the surface in memory of fourteen American and Soviet space explorers who had died during their nations' space programs.
What happened after deployment?
- The ALSEP central station was switched on at 18:37 UT on 31 July 1971.
- The Apollo 15 ALSEP station was shut down with the other ALSEP stations on 30 September 1977.
- After the astronauts returned to the Command/Service Module, the Lunar Module was jettisoned.
- The supplied source records that the Apollo 15 Lunar Module impacted the Moon at 03:03:37 UT on 3 August 1971, at 26.36° N, 0.25° E.
Important distinction: The descent stage and surface experiments were left at the landing site. The jettisoned Lunar Module that later impacted the Moon is a separate event and should not be described as the descent stage.
Why this hardware matters
Apollo 15's surface equipment represents a more extensive scientific installation than Apollo 11's EASEP. The instruments investigated seismic activity, magnetic fields, solar wind, ions, the lunar environment and heat flow. The ALSEP transmitted scientific data for years after the astronauts' visit and was eventually shut down by command from Earth.

Story angles supported by the source
- A field laboratory assembled by astronauts on the lunar surface.
- A power generator converting heat from plutonium-238 decay into electricity.
- Multiple instruments connected to a central station, sending measurements back to Earth.
- A rover that extended the astronauts' reach across the lunar terrain.
- A memorial left on the Moon.
Do not claim from this source
- That the ALSEP is still transmitting data. The source states it was shut down in 1977.
- That the jettisoned Apollo 15 Lunar Module and the descent stage are the same object.
- That every item carried by Apollo 15 is documented in this extract as still present or in a known condition.
PART B — MARS
4. Mars Pathfinder — Carl Sagan Memorial Station and Sojourner
Mission overview
- Mission: Mars Pathfinder
- Launch: 4 December 1996
- Landing: 4 July 1997
- Landing site: Ares Vallis, Mars
- Mission end (last data transmission): 27 September 1997
- Source: NASA, Mars Pathfinder mission page.
NASA describes Mars Pathfinder as a technology demonstration that delivered an instrumented lander and the first free-ranging robotic rover to the Martian surface.
Hardware left on Mars
A. Mars Pathfinder lander — Carl Sagan Memorial Station

After landing, the Pathfinder lander was named the Carl Sagan Memorial Station in honour of astronomer Carl Sagan.

The lander carried instruments for scientific observations and engineering data. NASA reports that the lander and rover instruments investigated the Martian atmosphere, climate, geology, and the composition of rocks and soil.

B. Sojourner rover
- Name: Sojourner
- Mass: 10.6 kg (23 pounds)
- Role: A small, free-ranging rover deployed from the Pathfinder lander.
- Deployment: Sojourner rolled down the lander's rear ramp on the night of 5 July 1997 (late in Martian day/sol 2), according to NASA's mission page.
NASA reports that Sojourner operated for 83 days, compared with a planned seven-day mission. It acquired images and performed chemical, atmospheric and other measurements.

C. Landing system hardware

Pathfinder used a parachute and a system of airbags to cushion its landing. NASA's mission page describes the lander bouncing approximately 15 times before coming to rest. The landing system is relevant to the story of what the mission delivered to Mars; the supplied NASA page does not give a complete item-by-item account of the final resting condition of every landing component.
What did the mission return?
NASA reports that, from landing until the final data transmission on 27 September 1997, Pathfinder returned:
- Approximately 2.3 billion bits of information.
- More than 16,500 lander images.
- 550 rover images.
- More than 15 chemical analyses of rocks and soil.
- Extensive data on winds and other weather factors.
NASA says findings from the lander and rover investigations suggested that Mars had been warm and wet at some time in its past.
Why this hardware matters
Pathfinder demonstrated a lower-cost approach to delivering a lander and a mobile rover to Mars. The lander and Sojourner worked together: the lander provided a stationary science and communications platform, while the rover could move among nearby rocks and soil.

Story angles supported by NASA
- A stationary lander that became a memorial station.
- A small rover that greatly exceeded its planned seven-day operating life.
- A new airbag-based landing approach.
- A lander-rover partnership that returned images, chemical analyses and weather information.
Do not claim from this source
- That Sojourner is still operational. The mission's final data transmission was in September 1997.
- That every airbag or landing component has a separately confirmed present-day condition.
- That the mission conclusively proved ancient life on Mars. NASA describes evidence suggesting Mars was once warm and wet, not proof of life.
5. Viking 1 — Mars lander
Mission overview
- Mission: Viking 1
- Launch: 20 August 1975
- Mars orbit insertion: 19 June 1976
- Landing: 20 July 1976
- Landing site: Chryse Planitia (Golden Plain), Mars
- Lander communications ended: November 1982
- Orbiter mission ended: 7 August 1980
- Source: NASA, Viking 1 mission page.
Viking 1 was part of NASA's two-spacecraft Viking project. Each spacecraft consisted of an orbiter and a lander. The orbiter studied Mars from orbit; the lander conducted investigations from the surface.
Hardware left on Mars
A. Viking 1 lander

The Viking 1 lander touched down safely on Mars on 20 July 1976 and began returning images from the surface. It carried scientific instruments to photograph the surroundings, collect soil samples, investigate the soil and atmosphere, and conduct experiments related to the search for life.

NASA lists the lander's instruments as:
- Imaging system (two facsimile cameras).
- Gas chromatograph mass spectrometer (GCMS).
- Seismometer.
- X-ray fluorescence spectrometer.
- Biological laboratory.
- Weather instrument package measuring temperature, pressure and wind velocity.
- Remote sampler arm.
NASA's mission page reports that the lander returned images, collected surface samples and studied the Martian environment. The seismometer was inoperable.

B. Power system

NASA states that each Viking lander carried two SNAP-19 radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs). These supplied power using heat from radioactive decay.
Scientific work and results
- The lander's robotic arm collected soil samples and delivered them to the onboard biological laboratory.
- Viking conducted experiments designed to investigate possible signs of life.
- NASA reports that the experiments produced unexpected and puzzling chemical activity in the soil, but did not provide clear evidence of living microorganisms.
- The lander returned images and weather observations from the surface.
- NASA's mission page describes the mission's contribution to understanding Mars as a cold planet with a thin, dry carbon-dioxide atmosphere and evidence of ancient river beds and flooding.
What happened to the lander and orbiter?
- The Viking 1 lander continued operating until November 1982, when communications stopped after a faulty command was sent from Earth. NASA's Viking 1 page gives 11 November 1982 as the lander end date; another NASA Viking overview gives 13 November 1982 as the end date. This document retains the date shown on the Viking 1 mission page and records the discrepancy rather than silently reconciling it.
- The Viking 1 orbiter was shut down on 7 August 1980 after running out of attitude-control propellant.
Important distinction: The lander was on the Martian surface. The orbiter was a separate spacecraft in orbit around Mars; it should not be described as surface hardware left beside the lander.
Why this hardware matters
Viking 1 was a major early attempt to investigate Mars directly from its surface and test for possible signs of life. Its lander combined cameras, a robotic sampler, chemical analysis instruments, a biological laboratory and weather sensors. The mission produced important observations even though its biology experiments did not establish that life was present.

Story angles supported by NASA
- A robotic laboratory that arrived on Mars in 1976.
- A mechanical arm collecting soil and delivering it to onboard instruments.
- Experiments designed to investigate whether the Martian soil showed signs of life.
- A lander that continued sending information for more than six years.
- The distinction between a mission ending and the hardware physically disappearing: the lander remained on the surface after communication ended.
Do not claim from this source
- That Viking 1 discovered life on Mars.
- That the lander is still operating.
- That the Viking 1 orbiter is on the surface.
- That the exact current physical condition of the lander is known from the cited mission page.

7. Terminology to use carefully
- Left on the surface: Hardware intentionally or operationally remaining at a landing site after the crew or mission phase departed.
- Lander: The spacecraft or landing platform that reaches the surface.
- Descent stage: The lower part of an Apollo Lunar Module, used for landing and left on the Moon.
- Ascent stage: The upper Apollo Lunar Module section that carried astronauts back to lunar orbit.
- Orbiter: A spacecraft that operates in orbit around a world; it is not surface hardware.
- Rover: A mobile vehicle that travels across a planetary surface.
- Experiment package / science station: Instruments deployed to collect measurements after the landing mission.
- Mission ended / communications ended: The end of data return or operations; it does not mean the hardware vanished.

10. Use of this research in AFTERLIGHT
This document supplies factual building blocks for future storytelling. It does not define the website's user interface, navigation, story format, audio design, or database architecture.

When creating a story from these notes:
- Keep verified mission facts separate from creative narration.
- Use dates, measurements and mission outcomes accurately.
- Explain why each object or instrument was sent and what it measured.
- Make the distinction between surface hardware and orbiting spacecraft clear.
- Do not suggest that an inactive object is still communicating or performing science.
- Link each published story to its NASA source material so readers can check the underlying facts.
